Your child is screaming on the floor over the wrong color cup. Or hitting their sibling. Or refusing everything you ask. Or going quiet and blank when you try to talk to them.

It's easy to see that as bad behavior. But for young children, a lot of what looks like misbehaving is actually a nervous system that's overwhelmed.

## Kids can't think their way out of overwhelm

The part of the brain that helps us pause, think, and calm down is still developing in young children. When their nervous system senses danger or overload, it takes over before they can make a choice.

That means a child in a meltdown often can't "just calm down," even if they want to. Their body is in survival mode.

## Behavior can look like fight, flight, or freeze

- Fight: hitting, yelling, throwing, defiance, "no!"
- Flight: running away, hiding, avoiding, refusing to go places
- Freeze or shutdown: going blank, not answering, zoning out, seeming "lazy" or checked out
- Fawn: becoming overly good, quiet, or eager to please to avoid upset

## What can overload a young child's nervous system

- Being tired, hungry, or sick
- Too much noise, activity, or screen time
- Big changes, like a new sibling, a move, or a new school
- Tension or conflict in the home
- Not feeling seen or connected
- Holding it together all day at school or daycare

## Behavior is communication

When you see behavior as communication, the question changes from "How do I stop this?" to "What is my child's body telling me?"

Often the answer is something like: "I'm overwhelmed." "I don't feel safe." "I need you." "I can't hold this anymore."

Talk to your pediatrician if behavior changes suddenly, seems extreme, or you're worried about your child's development. Kids also borrow calm from the adults around them, so your own nervous system plays a big part. ## Why it keeps coming back

Most of the time, we don't know the real reason behind what's driving the trigger. We can guess, but the actual trigger is usually something we would never think of on our own. And until it's found, your body will keep reacting to your child's overwhelm with your own.

That's why you do a session. In a Release Core session, you gently activate the original trigger so it can be rewired, and your body communicates what the actual trigger is and where it started. You don't have to guess.

As a parent, you don't have to carry this on your own. First, you ask your nervous system whether there is an emotional connection to how you feel when your child melts down.
