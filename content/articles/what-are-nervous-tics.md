What are nervous tics? They're sudden, repeated movements or sounds, like blinking, sniffing, throat clearing, or shrugging, that are hard to stop and often get worse when you're stressed. (They're sometimes spelled "nervous ticks.")

A blink you can't stop. A throat clear that keeps coming back. A shoulder shrug, a sniff, a small sound. And it always seems worse when you're stressed.

People often call these "nervous tics." Here's what they are, and why your nervous system plays such a big part.

## What a tic is

A tic is a sudden, quick, repeated movement or sound that's hard to control. Many people feel an urge or a building pressure right before a tic, and a sense of relief right after.

- **Motor tics** are movements, like blinking, nose scrunching, head jerks, or shoulder shrugs.
- **Vocal tics** are sounds, like throat clearing, sniffing, humming, or words.

Tics are very common in childhood, and many fade with age. For some people, they stay or come back later in life. Tourette syndrome is one type of tic disorder, but you don't need Tourette's to have tics.

## What the research found

**Tics change with what's going on around you.** In 2008, Christine Conelea and Douglas Woods published a review in the Journal of Psychosomatic Research on how outside factors affect tics. They found that tics aren't fixed. They go up and down depending on context, including stress, emotions, being watched, and activities.

**Stress and anxiety are the most consistent triggers.** In 2024, researchers studied 74 adults with tic disorders and asked them about their tics across different stages of life, from middle school through college and work. Stress and anxiety were the triggers people reported most consistently, at every stage of life. Tics were worse during and after school in the school years, and worse in the evenings in adult years.

These studies rely on what people report, so they show patterns rather than proof. But the pattern lines up with what so many people with tics notice every day.

## What keeps the nervous system in fight or flight

When your nervous system is on alert, everything gets turned up, including the urge to tic. The more pressure your body feels, the louder the tics get.

For a lot of people, that pressure isn't just this week's stress. It's a body that learned a long time ago that it had to stay on guard. And when you spend all day holding tics in so no one notices, your body is working even harder, which is why tics often explode as soon as you're home and finally safe.

## Signs your tics are tied to stress

- Your tics get worse during stressful weeks
- They're worse when you're tired, anxious, or overwhelmed
- They flare in the evening or after school or work
- They get louder when someone points them out
- You spend a lot of energy holding them in around others

New tics, especially in adults, should be checked by a doctor. Tic disorders are best supported with professional care, and approaches like CBIT have strong research behind them.

## Common questions

**Can stress cause tics?** Stress doesn't cause tic disorders on its own, but it's one of the biggest things that turns tics up. Many people first notice tics, or notice them coming back, during a stressful season.

**What is the difference between a nervous tic and a twitch?** A tic usually comes with an urge you can feel building and a sense of relief after it happens, and you can sometimes hold it in for a while. A twitch, like an eyelid twitch, is a small muscle spasm you don't feel coming and can't hold back.

**Can adults get nervous tics?** Yes. Tics are most common in childhood, but adults can have them too, either continuing from childhood or starting later. New tics in adults should be checked by a doctor.

**Do nervous tics go away?** Many childhood tics fade by the late teens. For others, tics come and go with stress, sleep, and what's going on in life. Lowering the pressure on your nervous system often makes them quieter.
