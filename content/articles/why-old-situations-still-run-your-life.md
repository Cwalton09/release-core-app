It happened years ago. You've moved on, at least on paper. But the moment something reminds your body of it, the same feeling comes rushing back: the same knot in your stomach, the same urge to shut down, the same old reaction.

That's what I call an open loop.

## What an open loop is

An open loop is an old situation your nervous system never got to finish. Maybe you couldn't speak up. Maybe you couldn't leave. Maybe no one came to help. Your body learned something in that moment, and it has been running that lesson ever since.

Open loops don't feel like memories. They feel like the way you are:

- "I always freeze in conflict."
- "I can't relax until everyone else is okay."
- "I always end up doing everything myself."
- "I just get anxious when things are going well."

## Why time alone doesn't close them

You might expect old experiences to fade on their own. Some do. But when your nervous system stored something as dangerous, it keeps it ready, so it can protect you the next time.

That's why you can understand exactly what happened, talk about it, even forgive the people involved, and still react the same way. The understanding lives in your thinking mind. The loop lives in your body.

## Memories can change when they're revisited

Here's what's exciting. For a long time, scientists believed that once a memory was stored, it stayed fixed. Then a famous 2000 study by Karim Nader and colleagues showed something different in rats. When a fear memory was brought back up, it became changeable for a short window before it was stored again.

Researchers call this memory reconsolidation. A 2017 review by Lee, Nader, and Schiller describes this window as ideally placed for updating memories with new information. In other words, when an old memory is reactivated and the body has a new experience at the same time, the memory itself can be updated.

It's important to be honest: research on using this in therapy is still developing, and clinical results have been mixed. But it fits what so many people experience when an old situation finally stops having the same charge.

## Changing how your body sees the old situation

This is the heart of what Release Core does. In a session, you gently bring up the original situation, so the loop is open. Then your body receives what it needed back then: safety, a voice, choices, support.

The situation doesn't change. What changes is your body's perspective on it. It's no longer happening. You're no longer that age. You're safe now. When that lands, the loop can finally close.

## Why it keeps coming back

Most of the time, we don't know the real reason behind what's driving the trigger. We can guess, but the actual trigger is usually something we would never think of on our own. And until it's found, your body will keep running an old situation like it's still happening.

That's why you do a session. In a Release Core session, you gently activate the original trigger so it can be rewired, and your body communicates what the actual trigger is and where it started. You don't have to guess.

## How Release Core helps

First, you ask your nervous system whether there is an emotional connection to the old situation that still affects you. If there is, you ask your body to lead you directly to it, so you can start the rewire. You don't have to dig through years of your life on your own.

You find the age the pattern started, what was happening in your life back then, and what your nervous system learned from it.

Then you rewire it, with questions like "Can you show me what it feels like to know that situation is over, and I'm safe now?" A question gives your nervous system something to go looking for instead of something to argue with. In my experience, that's where the shift happens, and the old loop can finally close.
