You took a DNA test, or a practitioner ran a panel, and there it was: an MTHFR variant. Suddenly it seemed to explain everything. The anxiety. The low moods. The exhaustion. Maybe you switched to methylated vitamins and waited to feel different.

For some people it helps a little. For many, the anxiety and depression are still there.

That doesn't mean you did something wrong. It may mean the gene was never the whole story.

## What MTHFR actually is

MTHFR is a gene that helps your body make an enzyme used to process folate, a B vitamin. The most talked-about variant is called **C677T**.

It's extremely common. According to the CDC, more people in the United States have one or two copies of the C677T variant than don't have it at all. Roughly 1 in 10 people in North America carry two copies, called "TT."

The CDC also says people with MTHFR variants can still process all types of folate, including regular folic acid. Their blood folate levels run only slightly lower, about 16% lower in people with two copies.

## What the research found about mood

In 2007, researchers at the University of York published a large review in the *American Journal of Epidemiology* that pooled studies on MTHFR and mental health.

For depression, people with two copies of the variant (TT) had about **1.36 times the odds** of depression compared with people with no copies. That's a real but small link. Most people with the TT variant don't have depression, and most people with depression don't have the TT variant.

For anxiety, the evidence is even thinner. Reviews describe the link between MTHFR and anxiety as unclear, and there's no strong body of research showing the gene causes anxiety on its own.

## Why geneticists don't recommend routine testing

In 2013, the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics, the main professional group for medical geneticists in the U.S., published a guideline titled "Lack of evidence for MTHFR polymorphism testing."

They concluded that testing for these common variants has **minimal clinical usefulness** and recommended against ordering it as part of routine workups.

## So what else is going on?

A gene is a small dial. It doesn't decide how you feel on its own.

Anxiety and depression are also shaped by what your nervous system has learned. If your body learned early that the world isn't safe, that you have to stay on guard, or that your feelings weren't welcome, it can stay in a protective state for years. That protective state affects your mood, your sleep, your energy, and even your digestion.

That's why someone can fix their vitamins and still feel anxious. The supplement may support one piece of the puzzle. It doesn't change what the nervous system is still bracing for.

## Signs it may be more than a gene

- You've tried methylated vitamins and still feel anxious or low
- Your symptoms get worse during stressful seasons
- You feel on edge in certain places or around certain people
- Your anxiety started or spiked after a hard life event
- You feel calmer on vacation, even with the same genes and supplements
