If you live with an autoimmune condition, you may have wondered whether a hard season in your life had something to do with when it started.

Many people notice it. A divorce, a loss, years of caregiving, a childhood that never felt safe, and then, somewhere along the way, the diagnosis.

Researchers have been asking the same question. Here's what they've found.

## What the research found

**A study of more than a million people in Sweden.** In 2018, researchers published one of the largest studies on this question in JAMA. They followed 106,464 people diagnosed with stress-related disorders, such as PTSD and severe stress reactions, and compared them with over a million people without those diagnoses, and with their own brothers and sisters.

Over about 10 years, people with a stress-related disorder were about 36% more likely to develop an autoimmune disease. The link held up even when they were compared with their own siblings, who shared much of the same genetics and upbringing.

**Childhood stress and adult autoimmune disease.** A 2009 study in Psychosomatic Medicine looked at 15,357 adults who had answered questions about adverse childhood experiences, like abuse, neglect, or growing up around addiction or violence. Researchers then tracked hospitalizations for 21 autoimmune diseases over the following years. The more childhood adversity someone had experienced, the more likely they were to be hospitalized for an autoimmune disease as an adult.

## What these studies do and don't show

These studies show a strong link. They don't prove that stress alone causes autoimmune disease. Genetics, infections, hormones, and environment all play a part, and many people develop autoimmune conditions without any obvious stress at all.

But the pattern across large studies is hard to ignore: a body that has lived under long-term stress appears to be more vulnerable.

## Why stress affects the immune system

Your nervous system and immune system are in constant communication. When your body senses danger, your stress response releases hormones like cortisol and adrenaline. In a short burst, that's helpful.

The problem is when the stress response doesn't shut off. Research suggests that long-term stress can make the body less responsive to cortisol's calming effect on inflammation. Your body keeps sounding the alarm, but it loses some of its ability to turn inflammation back down. Ongoing inflammation is one of the threads that runs through autoimmune disease.

## What keeps the nervous system in fight or flight

For a lot of people, the stress isn't only what's happening now. It's what the body learned a long time ago.

If you grew up in a home that felt unpredictable, or lived through something your body couldn't escape, your nervous system may have learned that it always has to be ready. That lesson doesn't fade just because life gets calmer. Your body keeps bracing, scanning, and holding, sometimes for decades, without you consciously feeling stressed at all.

That's what we mean when we say the nervous system is stuck in fight or flight. It's not a mood. It's a body that never got the message that the danger is over.

## Signs your body may be living in long-term stress

- You feel wired and exhausted at the same time
- You rarely feel fully relaxed, even on good days
- Your symptoms flare during or after stressful seasons
- You had a lot of stress or instability growing up
- You get sick or crash as soon as you finally slow down

These signs aren't a diagnosis. Autoimmune disease needs care from a doctor, and nothing here replaces that. Keep working with your medical team.
