You wake up, but you can't move. You can't speak. Your chest feels heavy. Maybe you sense someone standing in the room, or see a shadow at the foot of the bed.

Then, after a few seconds that feel like forever, it lets go.

If you've had sleep paralysis, you know how terrifying it is. And if it keeps happening, you may be scared to go to sleep at all.

## What sleep paralysis is

During REM sleep, the dreaming stage, your brain switches off most of your muscles so you don't act out your dreams.

Sleep paralysis happens when you wake up partway out of REM sleep. Your mind is awake, but your muscles are still switched off. Pieces of the dream can spill into waking, which is why so many people see or sense something in the room.

It's not dangerous on its own. But it can feel like the most frightening thing that's ever happened to you.

## What the research found

In 2011, Brian Sharpless and Jacques Barber published a review in *Sleep Medicine Reviews* that pooled 35 studies with more than **36,000 people**.

They found sleep paralysis is much more common than most people think:

- About **8%** of people in the general population have had it
- About **28%** of students
- About **32%** of people seeing a mental health professional
- About **35%** of people with panic disorder

Researchers noticed that sleep paralysis often goes along with stress, trauma, anxiety, poor sleep, and irregular sleep schedules, like shift work, jet lag, or all-nighters.

## Where the nervous system comes in

A body that's on high alert sleeps more lightly and wakes more easily, including out of REM sleep. And when you wake up frozen, your survival system sounds every alarm it has. That's why the experience is so often filled with fear and a sense of threat.

Then the fear of it happening again makes it harder to relax into sleep, which can make it more likely to happen.

## Signs your nervous system may be part of it

- Episodes come during stressful seasons
- You've had a lot of anxiety, panic, or trauma
- You're not sleeping enough, or your sleep schedule is all over the place
- You're scared to fall asleep
- You often wake up with a racing heart

Frequent episodes, falling asleep suddenly during the day, or episodes with other symptoms deserve a visit to a doctor or sleep specialist.
