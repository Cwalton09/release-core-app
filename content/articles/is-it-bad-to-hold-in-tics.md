If you have tics, you've probably gotten really good at holding them in. In a meeting. In class. On a date. Anywhere you don't want people to notice.

And you've probably noticed what it costs you. The urge builds, your focus slips, and later you feel completely drained.

So is it bad to hold in a tic?

## Holding in a tic is possible, but it takes effort

Many people can hold back a tic for a while. But the urge underneath doesn't just disappear. It often feels like pressure, an itch, or tension that keeps building until the tic finally comes out.

Holding it in isn't dangerous in itself. But it takes a lot of energy, and it can't be kept up forever.

## Why it's so exhausting

When you hold in a tic, part of your attention is always on your body. You're monitoring the urge, watching who's around, and bracing to keep it contained.

That's a lot of work for your nervous system. It's like running a background program all day long. No wonder you feel wiped out afterward.

## The release afterward

Many people find that once they're alone or somewhere safe, the tics come out more strongly than before. That's one reason tics can seem [worse in the evening](/articles/why-do-my-tics-get-worse-at-night) or right after school or work.

## Holding in vs. learning to manage tics

Constantly forcing tics down is different from tic-specific behavioral therapy. Therapies designed for tics teach people to notice the urge early and respond in a different way, without the constant strain. If you'd like support with that, ask your doctor about behavioral therapy for tics.

## The pressure behind the holding

For many people, the real weight isn't the tic itself. It's the fear of being seen, judged, teased, or misunderstood. That fear keeps the nervous system on guard, and a guarded nervous system tends to tic more. You can read more about [how tics and the nervous system are connected](/articles/tics-and-the-nervous-system).

## Why it keeps coming back

Most of the time, we don't know the real reason behind what's driving the trigger. We can guess, but the actual trigger is usually something we would never think of on our own. And until it's found, your body will keep bracing to hold everything in.
