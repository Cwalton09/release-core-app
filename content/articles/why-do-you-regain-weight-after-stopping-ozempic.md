Why do people regain weight after stopping Ozempic? In the clinical trials, most people regained a large share of their lost weight within a year of stopping. In the STEP 1 trial extension, people who stopped semaglutide regained about two-thirds of the weight they had lost. That's not a personal failure. These drugs quiet hunger signals while you take them, but they don't change the reasons your body was holding onto weight in the first place, and the body actively works to win lost weight back.

Many people describe the same thing: the "food noise" goes quiet on the medication, the weight comes off, and then after stopping, the hunger, cravings, and old patterns come roaring back.

## Talk with your doctor first

Weight-loss medications are real medical treatment and help many people, especially with diabetes, heart risk, and other conditions. Never stop or change a medication without your doctor. Doctors increasingly see obesity as a long-term condition, and the research suggests many people need ongoing treatment to keep the benefits. This article is about the part a medication can't reach, which can sit alongside your medical care.

## What the trials show

**Semaglutide (Wegovy, Ozempic).** In the STEP 1 trial extension, people lost about 17% of their body weight over 68 weeks. One year after stopping, they had regained about two-thirds of what they lost, and improvements in blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol moved back toward where they started.

**Tirzepatide (Zepbound, Mounjaro).** In the SURMOUNT-4 trial, people who were switched to a placebo after 36 weeks regained about 14% of their weight over the next year. People who stayed on the medication kept losing.

**A 2026 review.** A systematic review of 16 studies found weight regain is common after stopping these medications, though how much varies. The authors noted that strategies to prevent regain are still not well studied.

## Why the body pulls weight back

After weight loss, the body defends its old weight. A well-known 2011 study in the *New England Journal of Medicine* found that a year after weight loss, people still had higher levels of the hunger hormone ghrelin, lower levels of fullness hormones, and more hunger than before. Your body treats weight loss like a threat to survival.

GLP-1 medications override those hunger signals while you take them. When you stop, the signals return, often stronger.

## What the medication doesn't change

From a nervous system point of view, weight isn't only about appetite. Your nervous system also shapes:

- **Stress eating.** Stress hormones increase cravings for sugar and fat. See [why I eat when stressed](/articles/why-do-i-eat-when-stressed).
- **Cortisol and belly fat.** Long-term stress is linked to more fat around the middle. See [stress, weight, and cortisol](/articles/stress-weight-cortisol).
- **Sleep.** Poor sleep raises hunger hormones.
- **Food as comfort or safety.** If food is how your body learned to calm down, that need is still there after the medication stops.
- **Weight as protection.** For some people, research suggests weight became a kind of shield after hard experiences. See [can your body hold weight for protection](/articles/can-your-body-hold-weight-for-protection).

A medication can turn down hunger. It doesn't teach your nervous system that it's safe now.

## Signs your nervous system may be part of the regain

- Cravings came back strongest during stressful times
- You eat to calm down, numb out, or get through the evening
- You felt anxious or exposed as the weight came off
- You've lost and regained weight many times before
- You had a hard childhood or a frightening experience before weight gain started

## Common questions

**Is it normal to gain weight after stopping Ozempic?** Yes. Weight regain is common in the research and is not a sign you did something wrong.

**Can I keep the weight off after stopping?** Some people keep part of it off, especially with exercise and support. Talk with your doctor about a plan before stopping.

**Why does food noise come back?** The medication was quieting hunger and reward signals. When it stops, those signals return, along with any stress or emotional patterns that drove eating before.

## Why it keeps coming back

The real root is often very different from what you think. It usually isn't willpower or the medication. It's an old rule your body learned long before, like *Food is the only thing that makes me feel okay* or *It isn't safe to be seen*, and your body keeps following it no matter what the scale says.
