Why should you say what you want instead of what you don't want? Because your nervous system and your brain's filter focus on whatever you name. When you say "I don't want to be poor," the word your body hears loudest is "poor." Saying "I want to be extremely successful" points it toward what you actually want.

Think about how often you say what you don't want:

"I don't want to be broke anymore."

"I don't want to get sick."

"I don't want to end up like my parents."

"I don't want to mess this up."

It feels like you're setting a goal. But listen to what each sentence is actually about. Broke. Sick. Like my parents. Messing it up.

## Your brain can't picture "not"

Try this: don't think of a pink elephant.

You just pictured a pink elephant. Your brain has to picture the thing first in order to understand "don't" think of it.

The same thing happens when you say "I don't want to be poor." To make sense of the sentence, your mind and body have to bring up "poor": the feeling, the fear, the memories. You end up practicing the exact feeling you're trying to get away from.

## Your filter goes looking for what you name

Your brain has a filter called the reticular activating system. It helps decide what you notice, and it tends to let through whatever you keep focusing on.

So when you keep naming what you don't want, you're telling the filter what's important. And it shows you more of it: more bills, more worry, more proof that you're right to be afraid. I explain the filter more in [why your affirmations aren't working](/articles/why-affirmations-dont-work).

## It's not just words, it's your nervous system

Here's the part most people miss. When you say "I don't want to be poor," your body doesn't just hear it. It feels it. Your chest tightens. Your stomach drops. Your nervous system goes on alert, because "poor" is a threat to it.

Do that every day, and you're putting your body through a small dose of the thing you fear, over and over. That keeps your nervous system braced and focused on danger, which is the opposite of the calm, open state where good things are easier to notice and say yes to.

## How to flip it

Take what you don't want and ask: "So what do I want instead?"

- "I don't want to be poor" becomes "I want to be extremely successful and feel calm with money"
- "I don't want to get sick" becomes "I want my body to feel strong and healthy"
- "I don't want to mess this up" becomes "I want to feel confident and steady doing this"
- "I don't want to fight anymore" becomes "I want to feel peaceful and close with the people I love"

Notice how your body feels with each version. The "want" version usually feels lighter, even if part of you doesn't fully believe it yet.

## When your body still won't believe the new words

Sometimes you flip the sentence and your body still says "yeah, right." That's a sign your nervous system learned something long ago that says the thing you want isn't safe or isn't for you.

That's where questions come in. Instead of forcing the new sentence, you ask: "Can you show me what it feels like to be extremely successful and safe?" Your body goes looking for the feeling instead of arguing. Read more about [asking your body questions instead of saying affirmations](/articles/asking-your-body-questions-instead-of-affirmations).

## Common questions

**Why should you focus on what you want instead of what you don't want?** Your mind has to picture the thing you name, even when you say "don't." Focusing on what you want keeps your attention, your brain's filter, and your nervous system pointed toward it instead of toward what you fear.

**Does saying negative things really affect your body?** Many people notice their body tense up when they say what they're afraid of. Repeating fears keeps the nervous system on alert, which can make it harder to feel calm and open.

**What if I don't know what I want?** Start with what you don't want, then ask "what would I want instead?" The answer is usually the opposite feeling. You don't need the full picture yet, just the direction.
