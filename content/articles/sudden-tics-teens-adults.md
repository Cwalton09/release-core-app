The tics came out of nowhere. One week everything was normal, and the next there were jerks, sounds, or words you couldn't control. Maybe it's your teenager. Maybe it's you.

Sudden tics at an older age can be frightening. They're also more common than they used to be, and researchers have been paying close attention.

## What the research found

In 2021, Tamara Pringsheim and doctors from eight Tourette syndrome clinics in five countries published a report in the journal Movement Disorders. They described a sharp rise in young people, mostly girls and women ages 12 to 25, who suddenly developed complex movements and sounds that looked like tics.

At these clinics, these cases went from about 1 to 5% of new referrals before the pandemic to around 20 to 35%.

The doctors noted that these tic-like behaviors were different from typical childhood tics. They started suddenly, later in life, and were usually more complex. Many of the young people also had anxiety or depression. The researchers pointed to the stress and isolation of the pandemic, along with heavy social media use, as likely contributors.

Most of these cases were consistent with a functional neurological disorder. That means the nervous system's wiring is healthy, but the way it's working has changed. These symptoms are real and involuntary. They are not "faked," and they are not "just in your head."

## What this means

Sudden tics in teens and adults can have different causes, and some need medical testing. But this research shows how strongly stress and emotional overload can show up as movement in the body, especially in a nervous system that's already under pressure.

## What keeps the nervous system in fight or flight

When a nervous system is overloaded for a long time, it has to release that pressure somewhere. For some people it shows up as panic. For others, it's stomach problems, pain, or shutdown. For some, it comes out as movement.

The overload often isn't just today's stress. It can sit on top of things the body learned long ago: that it isn't safe to be seen, that it has to hold everything together, that it can't let go. When there's too much to hold, the body finds its own way out.

## Signs stress may be part of sudden tics

- The tics started suddenly, during or after a stressful season
- There's also anxiety, depression, or a lot of pressure going on
- The tics get worse with stress and better with rest
- They started after a lot of time isolated or online
- The person feels overwhelmed or like they're "holding it all in"

Anyone with new or sudden tics should see a doctor, ideally a neurologist or a tic disorder specialist. Functional tic-like behaviors respond well to the right support, so getting a clear diagnosis matters.
