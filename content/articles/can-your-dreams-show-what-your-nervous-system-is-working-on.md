Can your dreams show what your nervous system is working on? Often, yes. Sleep researchers have found that the brain keeps working through emotional experiences while you sleep, especially during REM sleep, when most vivid dreams happen. So your dreams tend to carry the same feelings, fears, and patterns your body is processing. When you're changing an old pattern, your dreams often change with it.

I see this all the time. After a Release Core session, people start having dreams that look a lot like the exact thing we worked on.

## Your brain processes emotion while you sleep

In a 2011 study at UC Berkeley, researchers led by Els van der Helm and Matthew Walker showed people emotional images, then showed them the same images 12 hours later. People who slept in between, and got REM sleep, had less reaction in the amygdala, the brain's alarm center, and rated the images as less intense. People who stayed awake didn't get that same drop.

Walker has described REM sleep as a kind of overnight therapy. The memory stays, but the emotional charge softens.

Dream researchers have also found that dreams tend to carry the emotional tone of waking life. The stronger the feeling during the day, the more likely it is to show up at night. This idea is often called the continuity hypothesis, and researchers still debate how far it goes.

## Dreams speak in feelings, not facts

Your nervous system doesn't think in words. It thinks in sensations, emotions, and patterns. So your dreams don't usually say "you're afraid of being abandoned." They show you a scene that feels like it.

That's why I don't use dream dictionaries. A snake, a house, or a test means different things to different people. What matters is how the dream felt and what role you were playing in it. Were you scrambling? Hiding? Rescuing? Being rescued?

## What it looks like after a session

One woman I worked with had a session around a rule her body had carried for years: *I have to handle everything myself. I can't depend on anyone.*

Two nights later, she dreamed she was back in school and hadn't done her homework. She was scrambling to find the answers on her own. Then her best friend handed her paper over and let her use it.

The next night, she dreamed something went wrong that she couldn't fix, and she panicked. But someone she trusted stayed calm, knew what to do, and took care of it.

Both dreams start with the old rule: I'm on my own. And both end with the new one: someone can help me, and I'm safe.

## Common questions

**Do dreams have meanings?** Researchers don't agree on one meaning for any dream. But many find that dreams reflect the emotions and concerns you're carrying. Reading them by feeling, not by symbol, tends to be the most useful.

**Why do I dream more after emotional work?** Strong emotional experiences give your brain more to process overnight, so dreams can get more vivid for a while. See [why am I having vivid dreams after healing work?](/articles/vivid-dreams-after-healing-work)

**Should I worry about a bad dream after a session?** Usually not. A dream that shows the old fear is often your body showing you what it's still protecting you from. If nightmares are frequent or disrupt your sleep, talk with a doctor.

## Why it keeps coming back

Recurring dreams usually repeat because the rule underneath hasn't changed yet. Your nervous system keeps running the same scene. And just like waking patterns, we usually don't know which rule a dream is pointing to on our own.
