How does the nervous system work? Your nervous system constantly takes in information from your body and your surroundings, compares it to what it has learned before, and decides how your body should respond. Most of that happens automatically, below your awareness. By the time you notice a feeling, your nervous system has usually already made its call.

That last part is the piece most people never hear. Your nervous system isn't waiting for you to decide how to feel. It's deciding first, and then you feel it.

## The basic parts

Your nervous system has two main parts.

- **The central nervous system** is your brain and spinal cord. It's the control center.
- **The peripheral nervous system** is the network of nerves running out to every part of your body. It carries messages back and forth.

Within that network is the **autonomic nervous system**, the part that runs things you don't consciously control, like your heart rate, breathing, digestion, and sweating. You can read more in [what does the nervous system control?](/articles/what-does-the-nervous-system-control)

## Gas pedal and brake

The autonomic nervous system has two main branches that work like a gas pedal and a brake.

- **The sympathetic branch** is the gas pedal. It gets you ready for action: faster heartbeat, tighter muscles, quicker breathing, sharper focus. This is the fight-or-flight response.
- **The parasympathetic branch** is the brake. It helps you rest, digest, recover, and connect. The [vagus nerve](/articles/what-is-the-vagus-nerve) is a big part of this side.

A healthy nervous system moves between the two all day. You speed up when you need to and settle back down when the moment passes.

## It's always asking one question

Underneath everything, your nervous system is asking: **am I safe right now?**

Researcher Stephen Porges coined the word *neuroception* for this constant, automatic scanning for safety and danger. It happens without thought. Your nervous system reads tone of voice, faces, sounds, body sensations, and situations, and it decides how to respond before your thinking brain gets a vote. More on that in [what is neuroception?](/articles/what-is-neuroception)

## It learns from experience

Here's what makes the nervous system so powerful, and so stubborn. It learns.

When something hurt you, scared you, or overwhelmed you, especially when you were young, your nervous system made a note. *This is dangerous. Next time, react faster.* Over time, those notes become rules. A raised voice means trouble. Resting means falling behind. Being seen means being judged.

Those rules are stored as patterns your body runs automatically, not as sentences you can read. That's why you can react strongly to something and honestly not know why.

## Why it keeps running old patterns

Your nervous system doesn't check the date. If it learned a rule at seven, it can keep running that rule at forty, long after the danger is gone.

So you end up with a body that braces in situations your mind knows are fine. You know the meeting is safe. You know your partner isn't your parent. You know you're allowed to rest. And your body reacts anyway. That's covered more in [why does my body still react when I know I'm safe?](/articles/why-body-reacts-when-you-know-you-are-safe)

## Common questions

**How does the nervous system send messages?** Nerve cells, called neurons, pass signals to each other using electrical impulses and chemical messengers called neurotransmitters. Those signals travel between your brain, spinal cord, and the rest of your body very quickly.

**Can you control your nervous system?** You can influence parts of it, like slowing your breath. But most of what it does, including how it reacts to perceived danger, happens automatically and is based on what it has learned.

**What happens when the nervous system is dysregulated?** It gets stuck leaning too hard on the gas, the brake, or both. That can show up as anxiety, exhaustion, poor sleep, digestive trouble, or feeling shut down. See [dysregulated nervous system symptoms](/articles/dysregulated-nervous-system-symptoms).
