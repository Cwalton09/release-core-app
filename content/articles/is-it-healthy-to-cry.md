Some people cry at every commercial. Others haven't cried in years, even when something truly awful happened. Some people feel better after a good cry. Others feel worse, or embarrassed, or like they'll never stop once they start.

So is crying actually good for you?

## What the research found

In 2014, Asmir Gračanin, Lauren Bylsma, and Ad Vingerhoets published a review in *Frontiers in Psychology* called "Is crying a self-soothing behavior?"

They pulled together the research on what crying does to the body and concluded there's growing evidence that crying can directly help soothe us, not only by bringing other people closer for comfort, but by calming our own bodies.

They proposed that crying may help by:

- Switching on the **parasympathetic nervous system**, the "rest and digest" side that helps you settle after stress
- Raising **oxytocin**, a hormone tied to comfort and connection
- Helping you see a situation differently once the wave passes

The authors described this as a likely mechanism, and research on it is still growing. Their later work found that whether crying helps depends a lot on the situation and on whether you feel supported.

## Why some people can't cry

If crying can help the body settle, why do so many people feel blocked?

Often, it's because their nervous system learned that crying wasn't safe. Maybe you heard "stop crying or I'll give you something to cry about." Maybe crying got you teased, ignored, or punished. Maybe you had to be the strong one.

When that happens, the body learns to hold the tears back. And holding them back takes effort, effort that can show up as a tight throat, a heavy chest, headaches, or a feeling of numbness.

## Signs your body is holding back tears

- A lump in your throat that won't go away
- You feel numb when you "should" feel sad
- You only cry when you're alone, or not at all
- You apologize when you cry
- You cry at movies, but not about your own life
- When you do cry, it feels like it might never stop
