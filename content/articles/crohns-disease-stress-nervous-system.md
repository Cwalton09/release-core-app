You're doing everything right. You take your medication. You know your trigger foods. And then a stressful month hits, and your gut falls apart again: the cramping, the urgency, the exhaustion, the planning your whole day around a bathroom.

If you live with Crohn's disease, you've probably noticed that stress and flares seem to travel together. You're not imagining it.

## What Crohn's disease is

Crohn's disease is a type of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). The immune system attacks the lining of the digestive tract, most often the end of the small intestine and the colon. It can cause pain, diarrhea, fatigue, weight loss, and complications that need real medical care. Stress doesn't cause Crohn's. Genes, the immune system, and gut bacteria all play a part.

But once you have it, your nervous system can turn the volume up or down.

## What the research found

**Stress and flares.** Researchers in Manitoba followed 552 people with IBD for a year. Of everything they looked at, including antibiotics, anti-inflammatory painkillers, and infections, **high perceived stress was the trigger most strongly linked to symptom flares.**

**Stress and symptoms, even without more inflammation.** A 2015 study of 478 people with IBD found that higher stress went along with **more symptoms**: more pain, more urgency, more trips to the bathroom. In that study, it wasn't tied to higher inflammation on stool tests. Your body feels more when your nervous system is on alert, and those symptoms are just as real.

**The vagus nerve and Crohn's.** Your vagus nerve, the main nerve of your "rest and digest" system, helps control inflammation in the gut. In a small French pilot study, people with active Crohn's had a device implanted to stimulate the vagus nerve. **After a year, five of the seven people were in clinical remission.** It was a tiny study, and bigger trials are still needed. But it shows your nervous system has a direct line to the inflammation in your gut.

## What this means

When your body stays in fight or flight, the "rest and digest" side of your nervous system has less of a say. That's the side that helps your gut heal, move food normally, and keep inflammation in check.

So a nervous system that never fully relaxes isn't just making you feel stressed. It can make your gut more sensitive, make symptoms louder, and leave less of the natural brake that keeps inflammation calm.

## Signs your nervous system may be part of your flares

- Flares tend to follow stressful weeks, big changes, or emotional events
- Your gut reacts before big conversations, travel, or events
- You feel on edge even when your Crohn's is calm
- You're always scanning for the nearest bathroom, even on good days
- You feel better during quiet seasons or on vacation

Never stop or change your Crohn's medication without your gastroenterologist. Severe belly pain, a fever, blood in your stool, or vomiting needs medical care right away.
