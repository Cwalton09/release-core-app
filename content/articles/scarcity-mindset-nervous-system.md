You can't stop running the numbers in your head. You forget appointments, make choices you regret, and can't seem to plan more than a week ahead. Then you blame yourself for not being "good with money."

There may be a reason your brain feels so crowded. And it isn't that you're bad at this.

## What the research found

In 2013, researchers from Princeton, Harvard, and the University of Warwick published a study in the journal *Science* called "Poverty Impedes Cognitive Function."

In one experiment, people were asked to think about a money problem, like an expensive car repair, and then took reasoning tests. For people with lower incomes, just thinking about the big bill lowered their scores. People with higher incomes weren't affected.

In a second study, the team tested 464 sugarcane farmers in India, who get most of their yearly income at harvest. The same farmers scored worse on thinking tests *before* the harvest, when money was tight, than *after* it, when they'd been paid.

The researchers' explanation: money worries take up mental "bandwidth." When part of your mind is always on what you can't afford, there's less left for everything else. The researchers compared the effect to losing about 13 IQ points, or to going a full night without sleep.

## Why it's more than a mindset

The word "mindset" makes it sound like a choice, as if you could just think abundant thoughts instead. But the study shows scarcity is something that happens *to* your brain. It pulls your attention whether you want it to or not.

And for many people, the sense of "not enough" doesn't go away when the money comes in. That's because the nervous system learned scarcity long before you had a bank account. If you grew up in a home where there was never enough, or where money was always an emergency, your body may still be scanning for the next shortage.

## Signs your nervous system learned scarcity

- You feel like there's never enough, even when there is
- You hoard, or you spend everything as soon as it arrives
- You can't plan ahead because you're stuck in "right now"
- You feel panicky about small, unexpected costs
- Rest, gifts, or nice things feel undeserved
- Your mind keeps returning to money, even when you try to relax
