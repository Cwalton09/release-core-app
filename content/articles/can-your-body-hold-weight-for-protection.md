Can your body hold weight for protection? For some people, yes, weight can become linked with feeling safe. This idea comes from real research. In the 1980s, Dr. Vincent Felitti ran an obesity program in San Diego. He noticed that many patients who lost large amounts of weight dropped out and quickly regained it. When he asked why, many described histories of abuse and said the weight made them feel protected or less noticed. That work led to the Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) Study.

Not everyone with extra weight has trauma, and weight has many causes, including genetics, medications, hormones, and environment. But if losing weight has ever felt strangely scary, this may be part of your story.

## Please get support

If you're dealing with memories of abuse, a trauma-informed therapist can help. If you're in crisis, call or text 988 in the US. Weight and eating concerns deserve care from a doctor, and if you feel out of control around food or restrict food, talk with an eating disorder specialist.

## What research shows

**Felitti's 1993 study.** Felitti compared 100 people entering a weight-loss program with 100 people who had always been slim. The patients in the weight program were much more likely to report childhood sexual abuse, other abuse, early loss of a parent, a parent with alcoholism, depression, and family problems. Many said they used weight as sexual protection, and many described overeating to cope with emotional pain.

**A 2014 meta-analysis.** A review in *Obesity Reviews* combined 23 studies with more than 112,000 people. Adults who reported childhood abuse were about 34% more likely to have obesity, and more severe abuse was more strongly linked. The authors pointed to stress, emotional coping, inflammation, and metabolism as possible reasons.

This research shows a link, not that weight is always about trauma.

## How weight can become "protection"

Your nervous system's job is to keep you safe. If being noticed, touched, or desired once led to harm, your body may have learned that being smaller or more visible is dangerous. Weight can feel like a buffer between you and the world.

Food can also become how the nervous system calms itself. When you've had to soothe yourself through hard things, eating may be the most reliable comfort your body knows.

## Signs weight may be tied to safety for you

- You feel anxious, exposed, or unsafe as weight comes off
- Compliments or attention after weight loss make you uncomfortable
- You regain weight around the time you start feeling noticed
- Weight gain started after a frightening or painful time
- You eat to numb out or feel grounded

## Common questions

**Can trauma cause weight gain?** Research links childhood adversity with a higher risk of obesity in adulthood, likely through stress, coping, and metabolism.

**Why do I self-sabotage my weight loss?** Sometimes the part of you that "sabotages" is trying to keep you safe. Understanding what it's protecting can change everything. See [why I self-sabotage](/articles/why-do-i-self-sabotage).

**Is it my fault?** No. Your body adapted to keep you safe. That's what nervous systems do.

## Why it keeps coming back

The real root is often very different from what you think. It usually isn't the food. It's an old rule your body learned long before, like *It's not safe to be seen* or *I need a wall between me and other people*, and the weight is doing a job for you.
