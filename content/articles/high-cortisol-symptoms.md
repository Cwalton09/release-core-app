Cortisol has become one of the most searched health words of the year. "Cortisol belly." "Cortisol face." "How to lower cortisol."

But cortisol isn't the villain. It's a messenger. And when it's running high for a long time, it's usually telling you something about your nervous system.

## What cortisol actually does

Cortisol is your main stress hormone. When your body senses danger, it releases cortisol to give you energy, raise your blood sugar, sharpen your focus, and help control inflammation. It also follows a daily rhythm, highest in the morning to help you wake up and lowest at night so you can sleep.

That's all helpful in short bursts. The trouble starts when the stress response doesn't switch off.

## What the research found

In 2012, Sheldon Cohen and his team at Carnegie Mellon published a study in PNAS on what long-term stress does to the body. They found that people who had been through ongoing, threatening stress developed glucocorticoid receptor resistance. Their bodies became less responsive to cortisol's signal.

Normally, cortisol helps turn inflammation down. But when the body stops listening to it, inflammation runs higher. In the study, people with this resistance were more likely to get sick when exposed to a cold virus, and produced more inflammatory chemicals when they did.

So long-term stress doesn't just mean "too much cortisol." It can mean a body that's stuck sounding the alarm while losing its ability to calm itself back down.

## What keeps the nervous system in fight or flight

Your body releases cortisol when it believes it needs to be ready. If your nervous system learned a long time ago that the world isn't safe, that you always have to be prepared, or that you can't let your guard down, it may keep that stress response running in the background every day.

You might not feel stressed. You might just feel like you never fully rest. But to your body, it's still an emergency, and the stress chemistry keeps flowing.

## Signs your body may be stuck in stress mode

- Wired at night, exhausted in the morning
- Trouble falling or staying asleep
- Cravings for sugar or salt
- Weight gain around the middle that's hard to shift
- Getting sick often, or crashing after stressful stretches
- Feeling anxious, irritable, or on edge for no clear reason

These signs can come from many things. If you're worried about your hormones, talk to your doctor. Conditions like Cushing's syndrome are rare but need medical testing.
