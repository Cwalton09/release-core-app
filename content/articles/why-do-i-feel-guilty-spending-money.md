You can afford it. You've been wanting it for months. You finally buy it... and the guilt hits before the package even arrives. Sometimes you return it. Sometimes you never buy it at all.

Meanwhile, spending on your kids, your partner, or your bills feels fine. It's only spending on *you* that feels wrong.

## What the research found

Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University and the University of Pennsylvania studied this in a 2008 paper in the *Journal of Consumer Research* called "Tightwads and Spendthrifts."

They found that people feel a real, emotional "pain of paying," and that some people feel it far more strongly than others.

- People they called "tightwads" felt so much pain when paying that they regularly spent *less* than they themselves wanted to
- The difference wasn't explained by income. People with plenty of money could still feel intense pain spending it
- Tightwads tended to have more savings, but researcher Scott Rick has said they were also less happy than people who didn't feel that conflict

Related brain-imaging research from Stanford and Carnegie Mellon found that seeing a high price activated the insula, a brain area involved in pain and disgust.

## Why it feels like guilt

That "pain" often shows up as guilt, because spending on yourself breaks a rule your body learned early. Maybe money was tight and you learned not to ask. Maybe someone was criticized or punished for spending. Maybe you learned that your needs came last.

So when you spend on yourself, your nervous system reacts as if you've done something risky or wrong, even when you haven't.

## Signs it's more than budgeting

- You feel guilty buying things you can clearly afford
- You spend easily on others, but not on yourself
- You return things you wanted, or leave them in your cart for weeks
- You feel you need to "earn" anything nice
- You hide purchases or downplay what they cost
- Rest, treats, or gifts to yourself feel undeserved
