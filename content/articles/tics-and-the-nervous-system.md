Maybe it's a blink, a throat clear, a shoulder shrug, a sniff, or a sound you can't hold back. Maybe it's been there since childhood, or maybe it showed up out of nowhere.

And maybe you've noticed something: it gets worse at certain times. Before a big event. During a stressful week. When you're exhausted. When someone points it out.

That isn't a coincidence. Tics and the nervous system are closely connected.

## What a tic is

A tic is a sudden, repeated movement or sound that's hard to control. Motor tics are movements, like blinking, grimacing, jerking your head, or shrugging. Vocal tics are sounds, like sniffing, throat clearing, coughing, humming, or repeating words.

Many people feel an urge right before a tic, like an itch, pressure, or tension that builds until the tic releases it. You might be able to hold it in for a while, but holding it usually makes the urge stronger.

Tics are common, especially in kids, and they can be part of conditions like Tourette syndrome or other tic disorders.

## Why stress makes tics worse

When your nervous system senses pressure, it shifts into a more activated state. Your muscles tighten, your heart speeds up, and your body gets more alert.

For many people with tics, that activation turns up the urge. That's why tics often flare:

- During stress, worry, or conflict
- Before tests, performances, or social events
- When you're overtired or run down
- When you're excited, not just upset
- When you're trying hard not to tic, or someone is watching
- After a long day of holding them in

And it's why they often ease when you're calm, focused on something you enjoy, or completely absorbed in an activity.

## Why tics come and go

Tics tend to wax and wane. They can be quiet for weeks and then suddenly get louder, or one tic can fade while a new one takes its place.

That pattern often lines up with what's going on in a person's life. A new school year, a move, a breakup, a loss, a stressful job, or a season where you feel unsafe or under pressure can all keep the nervous system more activated, and the tics can follow.

## The pressure of trying to stop

One of the hardest parts of having tics is how people react to them. Being told to "just stop," being teased, or feeling stared at can make you hyper-aware of every twitch or sound.

That awareness can turn into its own stress loop. You worry about the tic, your body tenses, the urge builds, and the tic gets stronger. Over time, your nervous system can start to treat certain places or people as unsafe, because that's where you felt the most judged.

## When to see a doctor

Tics have a neurological side, and they deserve medical care. Talk to your doctor if tics are new, changing quickly, painful, interfering with daily life, or came on suddenly in a child, especially after an illness. A doctor can help rule out other causes and talk with you about options, including behavioral therapy designed specifically for tics.
