You flush out of nowhere. Your skin itches or breaks out in hives. Foods you used to eat fine suddenly cause reactions. Smells, heat, and stress all seem to set something off. And every test comes back "normal."

If you've been told you might have mast cell activation syndrome, or MCAS, you've probably also noticed something else: your symptoms get worse when life gets stressful.

There's a reason for that. Mast cells and your nervous system are in constant contact.

## What MCAS is

Mast cells are immune cells found throughout your body, especially in your skin, gut, airways, and around blood vessels. When they sense a threat, they release chemicals like histamine. That's what causes the itching, swelling, and flushing of an allergic reaction.

In MCAS, mast cells release these chemicals too easily or too often.

Diagnosing it isn't simple. A widely used expert consensus, updated in 2021, says three things all need to be true:

- **Repeated symptoms in more than one part of the body**, like the skin, gut, and heart or airways at the same time
- **A lab sign of mast cell activation during an episode**, most often a rise in a blood marker called tryptase above your own baseline
- **Symptoms that improve** with medications that block or calm mast cell chemicals

Doctors don't all agree on the criteria. A competing set of guidelines is broader, and some specialists say many people referred for MCAS don't meet the strict definition. That's worth knowing, because it means your symptoms may be real even if the label is debated.

## Mast cells live next to your nerves

Research describes mast cells as messengers between the immune system and the nervous system. A 2021 review in *Neural Regeneration Research* noted that in the brain, mast cells sit especially close to nerve endings that release corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), one of the body's main stress hormones.

## What happens when stress hits

Researchers led by Theoharis Theoharides tested this in rats. After a stressful restraint test, **about 70% of the mast cells** in the lining around the brain released their contents **within 30 minutes**.

When the animals were first given an antibody that blocked CRH, that stress reaction was stopped completely.

Later research found human mast cells can also make CRH themselves, so they may respond to stress signals and send them out too.

Most of this research is in animals, so scientists are still working out exactly what it means for people with MCAS. But it points to a real pathway: when your body goes into stress mode, your mast cells are listening.

## Why your body may stay reactive

A nervous system that's always on alert keeps stress hormones running in the background. For mast cells that are already sensitive, that background stress can be one more push toward reacting.

That's why so many people with MCAS notice flares during hard seasons: a move, a breakup, a sick parent, a stressful job, a holiday with family.

## Signs your nervous system may be part of your flares

- Your symptoms flare during or right after stressful weeks
- You react more when you're exhausted or not sleeping
- Emotional moments are followed by flushing or hives
- You feel calmer and less reactive on vacation
- You feel on edge even when nothing is wrong

MCAS symptoms can be serious. If you have trouble breathing, swelling of your face or throat, or feel faint, use your emergency medication if you have one and call 911. Work with an allergist or immunologist for diagnosis and treatment.
