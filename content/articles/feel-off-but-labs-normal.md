You're tired all the time. Your stomach hurts. You get dizzy, achy, foggy. You finally see a doctor, get the bloodwork, maybe even scans.

And everything comes back normal.

You're relieved for a minute. Then frustrated. Because you still feel terrible, and now you're wondering if it's all in your head.

It isn't. And you're far from alone.

## What the research found

In 1989, Kurt Kroenke and Arthur Mangelsdorff published a study in *The American Journal of Medicine* that reviewed the records of **1,000 patients** at an internal medicine clinic over three years.

They looked at 14 of the most common reasons people see a doctor, including:

- Fatigue
- Dizziness
- Headache
- Chest pain
- Abdominal pain
- Back pain
- Numbness
- Insomnia

Doctors ran tests in more than two-thirds of cases. But they found a clear physical cause for only about **16%** of the symptoms. Treatment was given for only about half, and it often didn't help.

The researchers concluded that looking only for a physical disease isn't enough to explain most everyday symptoms.

## Normal labs don't mean nothing is wrong

Blood tests and scans are great at finding certain things: infections, anemia, thyroid disease, broken bones, blocked arteries.

But they don't measure how your nervous system is working. They can't show a body that's been running on high alert for years: muscles that stay braced, digestion that's slowed down, sleep that never gets deep, a heart rate that jumps at small things.

Those changes are real. They just don't show up on a standard lab report.

## How a stuck nervous system feels

When your body believes it's under threat, it shifts into protection mode. That can look like:

- Exhaustion that sleep doesn't fix
- Tension headaches and a tight jaw
- Stomach pain, bloating, or nausea
- Dizziness or lightheadedness
- Racing heart or chest tightness
- Brain fog and trouble focusing
- Aches that move around the body

None of these mean you're imagining things. They mean your body is doing exactly what it's built to do in danger, long after the danger has passed.

## Keep working with your doctor

Normal results are good news, but they're not always the end of the story. Some conditions take time or specialized testing to find. Keep following up, especially if symptoms change, get worse, or you have weight loss, fevers, or night sweats.
