# The Science of Why Content Goes Viral (applied to short-form video and health/wellness content)

Notes compiled 2026-11-12. Source-quality legend: [PR] = peer-reviewed; [LD] = large dataset (100k+ items); [IND] = industry/vendor data, unverified; [SEC] = secondary summary of a primary paper. Items marked "(recalled, not re-fetched)" are well-known papers whose details came from prior knowledge rather than a page fetched this session. Treat their exact figures as needing a quick check before you quote them publicly.

## 1. Berger & Milkman (2012): emotion, arousal, and what followed

### Takeaway
Berger & Milkman found that arousal, more than positive vs negative tone, predicts sharing. High-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) raise sharing and low-arousal sadness lowers it. Large studies since then (2017-2024) agree that emotional and especially negative or moral-emotional content spreads more. But a 2023 study of about 105k headlines found that sadness, not anger, drove clicks. So the "sadness hurts" finding may hold for sharing but not for attention or clicks.

### Cited Findings
- Berger & Milkman, *Journal of Marketing Research* 49(2), 2012 [PR][LD]: they studied about 7,000 NYT articles over 3 months and tracked which ones made the "most emailed" list, then ran lab experiments. Content that evokes high-arousal positive emotions (awe) or high-arousal negative ones (anger, anxiety) is more viral. Content that evokes deactivating emotions (sadness) is less viral. Positive content overall beat negative content, and the results held after controlling for surprise, interest, practical value and prominent placement. The experiments showed the effect is causal and runs through activation (arousal) — [Berger & Milkman PDF, Wharton](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf); [abstract record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1360292620129675648?lang=en)
- Effect sizes in the paper's logistic models, as odds of making the most-emailed list per 1 SD increase (recalled, not re-fetched; check Table 4 of the PDF above): awe about +30%, anger about +34%, anxiety about +21%, sadness about -16%, practical value about +30%, interest about +25%, surprise about +14% — [Berger & Milkman PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf)
- Companion experiment: Berger, *Psychological Science* 2011, "Arousal increases social transmission of information". Physiological arousal induced in an unrelated way (jogging in place) raised willingness to share neutral content (recalled, not re-fetched) — [Berger 2011, Psych Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797611413294)
- Brady, Wills, Jost, Tucker & Van Bavel, *PNAS* 2017 [PR][LD]: 563,312 tweets on gun control, same-sex marriage and climate change. Each moral-emotional word raised retweets by about 20%. Purely moral or purely emotional words did not. The boost stayed mostly within like-minded networks, with little spread across ideological lines — [NYU CSMaP summary](https://csmapnyu.org/research/academic-research/emotion-shapes-the-diffusion-of-moralized-content-in-social-networks); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/5514704)
- Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden, *PNAS* 2021 [PR][LD]: about 2.7M Facebook and Twitter posts from news media and US Congress members. Posts about the political out-group were shared about twice as often. Each out-group word raised the odds of a share by about 67%. Out-group language was about 4.8x as strong a predictor as negative-affect words and about 6.7x as strong as moral-emotional words. It also predicted "angry" reactions, while in-group words predicted "love" reactions (effect sizes via [SEC] summary) — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/8256037); [ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PNAS..11824292R/abstract); [summary](https://mindblog.dericbownds.net/2021/07/out-group-animosity-drives-engagement.html)
- Robertson et al., *Nature Human Behaviour* 2023 (Registered Report) [PR][LD]: about 105,000 Upworthy headline A/B variants, about 5.7M clicks and more than 370M impressions. Negative words raised click-through and positive words lowered it. Of the discrete emotions, **sadness** drove clicks and anger had no effect, which the authors flag as contrasting with the anger-and-sharing literature. This measures private clicks, not sharing — [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/03/negative-words-in-news-headlines-generate-more-clicks-but-sad-words-are-more-effective-than-angry-or-scary-ones/); [paper PDF](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/94951/1/s41562-023-01538-4.pdf)
- Watson, van der Linden, Watson & Stillwell, *Scientific Reports* 2024 [PR][LD]: 95,282 articles from 4 US/UK news sites matched to 579M Facebook and Twitter posts. Negative articles were about 1.91x more likely to be shared. The effect varied by topic (negative sports stories were shared slightly less) — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11405697/); [Cambridge JBS summary](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/2024/why-social-media-users-like-sharing-negative-news/)
- Schöne, Parkinson & Goldenberg (2021, *Affective Science*): negativity predicted the spread of tweets after both positive and negative political events (cited in Watson et al.) — [PMC11405697](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11405697/)
- Staiano & Guerini (viral images) added "dominance" as an emotion dimension. A secondary summary reports that low-arousal negative content can still spread if it is surprising (single study, [SEC]) — [HIVI Lux summary](https://hivi.uni.lu/?p=266)

### Inferences
- The overall picture is consistent: emotional intensity raises sharing. In political and news settings negativity and out-group hostility are the strongest levers, but they work mainly inside the in-group. For a wellness brand, "anger at an out-group" (doctors, Big Pharma, "toxic" people) would likely raise engagement but carries credibility and brand risk. The better-fitting levers are awe and relief, which are high-arousal positive emotions, plus anxiety that the post then resolves.
- Sadness may hook attention (Robertson) without driving shares (Berger & Milkman). For short video, a sad or relatable opener may stop the scroll, but the content probably needs a high-arousal or useful payoff to earn sends and shares.
- Most evidence comes from news and politics on Twitter and Facebook, not TikTok, Reels or wellness. Carry the findings over as directional, not as magnitudes.

### Gaps
- I found no 2020s meta-analysis that pools emotion and arousal effects on sharing. Searches turned up only primary studies and blogs, and one blog that claimed meta-analytic support was unreliable.
- I found no direct preregistered replication of Berger & Milkman on short-form video platforms.

## 2. STEPPS (Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value, Stories): the evidence

### Takeaway
STEPPS comes from Berger's popular book *Contagious* (2013). It is a synthesis, not a model that has been tested as a whole. Each part rests on separate studies, mostly Berger's own, plus his 2014 peer-reviewed review, which frames word of mouth as serving 5 functions: impression management, emotion regulation, information acquisition, social bonding and persuasion.

### Cited Findings
- Berger, "Word of mouth and interpersonal communication: A review and directions for future research", *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 2014 [PR]: word of mouth is goal-driven and serves 5 functions: impression management, emotion regulation, information acquisition, social bonding and persuasion. Audience and channel shift which function dominates — [record](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/901583329); [Berger WOM review PDF (Annual Rev Psych version)](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/WOM-Annual-Rev-of-Psych.pdf)
- **Social currency / identity:** impression management leads people to share identity-relevant content, and emotion regulation leads them to share emotionally intense content ([SEC] course notes) — [course notes](https://mike-marketing-research.share.connect.posit.cloud/06-virality.html)
- **Emotion:** see Section 1 (Berger & Milkman 2012; Berger 2011; Brady 2017).
- **Practical value:** in Berger & Milkman 2012, practical value independently predicted making the most-emailed list, controlling for emotion — [Berger & Milkman PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf)
- **Triggers:** Berger & Schwartz, *JMR* 2011, "What drives immediate and ongoing word of mouth?". Products that everyday cues bring to mind more often get more *ongoing* word of mouth, while interesting products get only *immediate* word of mouth (recalled, not re-fetched) — [JMR DOI](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.48.5.869). Also Berger & Fitzsimons, *JMR* 2008, "Dogs on the beach": cues in the environment raise how accessible a product is in memory, and so how often it is chosen and evaluated (recalled, not re-fetched) — [JMR DOI](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.45.1.1)
- **Stories / narrative:** in health short video, "authentic stories about people's lives" got far more engagement than a health professional giving an opinion (O'Donnell et al., *J Health Communication* 2023, n=400 #EduTok videos) — [WSU release](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2023/07/26/viral-tiktok-health-videos-tend-to-cover-three-topics-rely-on-influencers/)
- **Public / observability:** the MusicLab experiments (Section 5) show that making others' choices visible raises inequality in popularity, which is the mechanism behind "public" social proof — [Salganik et al. 2006 PDF](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/research/papers/years/2006/salganik2006a.pdf)

### Inferences
- For the Release Core content, the strongest-supported STEPPS levers are Practical value (a usable insight), Emotion (relief, awe, or anxiety that gets resolved) and Stories (anonymized session arcs). Triggers suggests tying posts to recurring daily cues, such as "the 3 a.m. wake-up" or "that tight-chest feeling before a call", so the content comes back to mind and gets sent on later.
- Social currency in wellness likely means "this makes me look caring or insightful when I send it to a friend". That is impression management plus social bonding.

### Gaps
- No study validates STEPPS as a complete framework or compares the relative weight of its six parts.
- I did not re-fetch the triggers papers this session, so their specific numbers are omitted.

## 3. Why people share health/wellness content

### Takeaway
People share health content to help others (practical, prosocial), to bond and get support, and to manage their identity. On TikTok specifically, engagement in health content follows relatable personal stories, role-model and lifestyle appeals and style, more than expertise or accuracy. The evidence on credentialed creators is mixed and drawn from small samples.

### Cited Findings
- O'Donnell, Jerin & Mu, *J Health Communication* 2023 [PR], 400 #EduTok health videos: almost 50% used "role model" appeals, which got the highest engagement. Personal, authentic stories beat a professional's opinion. Mental health was the most common topic but had relatively low engagement. Self-diagnosis videos (depression, anxiety) had very high engagement, which the authors call a problematic trend. Shock and scare videos drew views but often lacked facts — [WSU release](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2023/07/26/viral-tiktok-health-videos-tend-to-cover-three-topics-rely-on-influencers/)
- Douyin cancer narratives (*Frontiers in Communication* 2025, n=553) [PR]: "fighting cancer" narratives dominated in number, but **daily-life narratives got more engagement** — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2025.1634011/full)
- JMIR Mental Health 2025 (mental health medication on TikTok): videos by non-medical creators got significantly more views, likes and comments than those by verified medical professionals — [JMIR Mental Health](https://mental.jmir.org/2025/1/e77383/XML)
- On the other side: a JMIR study of breast-enhancement product videos found licensed physicians' videos got more likes than uncertified creators' videos. A 2026 JMIR Formative study (natural remedies, n=118) found the highest engagement for credentialed experts, but only n=2 such videos — [JMIR Formative 2026](https://formative.jmir.org/2026/1/e83274/XML)
- JMIR Infodemiology 2026 (depression on TikTok): engagement (likes + comments + saves + shares) "appears to be driven primarily by stylistic features rather than diagnostic accuracy" — [JMIR Infodemiology](https://infodemiology.jmir.org/2026/1/e85323)
- JMIR Dermatology 2025 (sunscreen on TikTok): engagement is often unrelated to accuracy or educational quality — [JMIR Dermatology](https://derma.jmir.org/2025/1/e70010)
- Chronic illness on TikTok (arXiv 2023, n=160 posts): 60% of posts were motivated by raising awareness, an altruistic or advocacy motive for self-disclosure — [arXiv 2311.11481](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.11481)
- Survey work: people with a personal or family history of disease are more likely to share health information (South Korea, 2017/2019). In online health communities, people weigh perceived benefits against costs before sharing (PLoS ONE 2023) — [PLoS ONE 2023 / PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10259799/); [BL record, Korea study](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100093215459_0x000001)

### Inferences
- For a wellness practitioner, "relatable lived experience plus one usable takeaway" fits the evidence better than "credentialed lecture". Anonymized client-session story carousels and Chelsea's first-person story fit the role-model and narrative pattern that performs best.
- Because engagement does not track accuracy in health content, accurate content will not be rewarded automatically. It has to compete on style, story and emotional resolution.
- The high engagement on self-diagnosis content is an ethical caution: it rewards "do you have X?" framing, which can over-pathologize.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous causal study of "send to a friend" (DM share) behavior for health content specifically. Platforms rarely expose DM-share data to researchers.
- Most TikTok health studies are cross-sectional with 100-500 videos, often keyword-sampled, so the results are correlational.

## 4. Attention and hooks in short video (first 1-3 seconds, curiosity, faces, captions, sound-off)

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed evidence on the precise swipe-decision window is thin. One systems study confirms that swipes cluster right after playback starts and near the end. The widely quoted "first 3 seconds" percentages come from vendor blogs with no traceable primary source. Large thumbnail datasets show that faces are common in breakout videos but give no clear advantage over non-face thumbnails.

### Cited Findings
- Dashlet (arXiv 2022, short-video streaming, TikTok-like swipe traces): users are most likely to swipe either soon after playback begins or at the tail end of the video. This supports the idea of a front-loaded decision but gives no exact threshold — [arXiv 2204.12954](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.12954)
- PaperTok (arXiv 2026, qualitative study of short research videos): effective hooks create "wait, what?" moments that need resolving, which is a curiosity gap. Participants consistently named short duration as key to retention — [arXiv 2601.18218](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.18218)
- [IND] Vendor figures such as "TikTok average 3-second retention is 32%; recommended videos 55%+" and "65% decide in the first 3 seconds" have no primary source. One analysis notes that the origin of the "+65%" figure is unclear — [grow-it](https://www.grow-it.se/en/resources/short-form-video-scripting-retention-hooks); [QuickReel](https://quickreel.io/blog/clip-hook-length-data)
- Faces in thumbnails [IND][LD]: Eden analyzed 507,478 YouTube videos and found 84.2% of breakout thumbnails had a face, versus 82.2% of underperformers, so almost no difference. Search Engine Journal and WhyShy (about 300k videos) also found face and no-face thumbnails performed similarly, with bigger differences by niche. vidIQ (2026, 500 breakouts) reports face use rising with breakout. YouGov found 72% of 740 popular videos had a face, but with no comparison group — [Eden](https://eden.so/blog/what-makes-a-good-youtube-thumbnail/); [SEJ](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/do-faces-help-youtube-thumbnails-heres-what-the-data-says/563944/); [vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-thumbnail-study/); [YouGov](https://yougov.com/articles/32864-72-popular-thumbnails-youtube-feature-human-face-a)

### Inferences
- What is well supported: front-load the payoff or tension, keep videos short, open a curiosity gap that the video closes, and design for the early-swipe danger zone. The exact "1.5 seconds" or "3 seconds" numbers are folklore-level.
- Faces are table stakes in many niches, not a reliable edge. Expression and clarity probably matter more than whether a face is there at all, and niche matters.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the reported CHI 2024 paper on the first 3 seconds of short video (Lin et al.), so its findings are unverified.
- I found no peer-reviewed data this session on on-screen captions or sound-off viewing rates. The often-cited "85% of Facebook video watched without sound" (Digiday, 2016) is dated, comes from industry, and was not re-verified. Treat it as unknown for TikTok and Reels in 2026.
- I found no eye-tracking studies of first frames in vertical short video in this search window.

## 5. Is virality predictable? Luck, cumulative advantage, volume, structural virality

### Takeaway
Virality is mostly unpredictable at the level of a single post. Social influence amplifies small random early differences (MusicLab). Even the best models explain less than half the variance in cascade size (Martin et al. 2016). More than 99% of cascades die within one generation, and true viral hits are about 1 in a million (Goel et al. 2016). Popularity usually comes from one big broadcast (a large account or algorithmic push), not from long chains of person-to-person spread. The practical consequence is that many attempts plus quality beat trying to engineer one hit.

### Cited Findings
- Salganik, Dodds & Watts, *Science* 311:854-856, 2006 (MusicLab) [PR]: about 14,000 participants (recalled; check the paper) spread across 8 "social influence worlds" plus an independent world. Showing download counts raised inequality (Gini) in all 8 worlds and made outcomes unpredictable across identical worlds. Stronger social signals produced more inequality and more unpredictability. Quality mattered only partly: the best songs rarely did badly and the worst rarely did well, but anything else was possible — [Salganik et al. PDF](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/research/papers/years/2006/salganik2006a.pdf); [Toronto copy](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~bor/303s19/docs/salganik_dodds_watts.pdf)
- Follow-up (Abeliuk et al.): ranking position affects unpredictability more than social influence does. This is relevant to algorithmic feeds — [arXiv 1408.1542](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1408.1542.pdf)
- Goel, Anderson, Hofman & Watts, *Management Science* 62(1):180-196, 2016 [PR][LD]: about 1 billion diffusion events on Twitter (news, videos, images, petitions). More than 99% of cascades are tiny and end within one generation, and viral hits occur at about 1 in a million. Structural virality is typically low, and size correlates only weakly with it, so popularity is driven mainly by the largest single broadcast. The authors describe a continuous range from broadcast to viral rather than a binary — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=215390); [paper PDF](https://5harad.com/papers/twiral.pdf)
- Martin, Hofman, Sharma, Anderson & Watts, WWW 2016, "Exploring limits to prediction in complex social systems" [PR][LD]: even with extensive data, the best models explain **less than half the variance** in Twitter cascade size. With unlimited data, predictive performance would still be bounded well below deterministic accuracy — [arXiv 1602.01013](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.01013)
- Juul & Ugander, *PNAS* 2021 [PR]: matching cascades on size, the structural differences between true and false news (Vosoughi et al.) almost entirely disappear, because false-news cascades are simply bigger. Differences across content types (videos, images, news, petitions) persist after controlling for size — [PMC8609637](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8609637); [Cornell news](https://news.cornell.edu/node/322860)

### Inferences
- With hits this rare (about 1 in a million cascades) and only about 50% of variance explainable, the rational strategy is high, consistent volume of decent-quality posts that test hooks and formats. Treat each post as a lottery ticket with odds you can improve, and judge performance on rolling averages, not single posts. This supports the existing max-2-per-day, consistent cadence approach.
- "Broadcast beats chains" means distribution events matter most: algorithmic pickup (For You page / Trial Reels), shares into large groups (for example the Facebook group), or a collaborator with a big audience.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study this session that links posting volume to the probability of a hit for individual creators. The volume inference rests on the base-rate and unpredictability findings, not on a direct test. (Simonton's "equal-odds rule" from creativity research is related but was not fetched.)
- All the cascade studies use Twitter. Algorithmic, interest-graph feeds like TikTok may raise the share of reach from "broadcast by algorithm" even further, but I found no published structural-virality analysis of TikTok.

## 6. Faces vs faceless, human voice vs text-to-speech, authenticity and parasocial trust

### Takeaway
Human voices are generally trusted more than synthetic ones when the speaker is trying to convey warmth or trustworthiness, and *believing* a speaker is human raises trust on its own. Parasocial relationships and perceived authenticity are central to health-influencer trust and persuasion. That same trust also lowers followers' scrutiny of the advice. Polished commercial content triggers skepticism.

### Cited Findings
- CHI 2022 experiment (n=79): a virtual human was rated less trustworthy with neural TTS than with recorded human speech — [Frontiers / CHI-related record](https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/conferenceProceeding/A-New-Uncanny-Valley-The-Effects/991021916517904721)
- Cognitive Science Society 2025: listeners hearing *identical* clips were more trusting and empathic, and donated and followed advice more, when told the speaker was human — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/8vr8s6h8)
- Maltezou-Papastylianou et al. (Essex, 2025): synthetic voices sounded *more* trustworthy than human voices when the speech had no intended tone, but *less* trustworthy when human speakers deliberately tried to sound trustworthy — [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/41541/)
- Han et al., *Psychology & Marketing* 2023 meta-analysis of influencer impact: source credibility is the most robust explanatory framework, with parasocial interaction also a key route — [City Univ open access PDF](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/31583/1/Psychology%20and%20Marketing%20-%202023%20-%20Han%20-%20Meta%E2%80%90analysis%20of%20social%20media%20influencer%20impact%20Key%20antecedents%20and%20theoretical.pdf)
- PRISMA review of 49 studies (2015-2026) on Gen Z and key opinion leaders/consumers: an "authenticity paradox" in which highly polished commercial content triggers skepticism — [HRMARS PDF](https://hrmars.com/papers_submitted/27647/from-authority-to-authenticity-a-systematic-review-of-gen-zs-divergent-responses-to-kols-and-kocs.pdf)
- Scoping review on prescription-drug influencers (2026, [SEC] reporting): followers form one-sided parasocial relationships, and emotional stories build trust and make followers less likely to question the medical advice — [Healthline report](https://www.healthline.com/health-news/prescription-drug-content-social-media-influencers-misleading); [Public Health Post](https://publichealthpost.org/mental-behavioral-health/familiar-faces-risky-choices-parasocial-relationships-and-health/)

### Inferences
- For a trust-based service (paid sessions), Chelsea's own voice and face, at least some of the time, should build the parasocial trust that converts. TTS is acceptable for neutral informational voiceovers but probably costs warmth. Disclosure effects suggest it is better not to pass off a synthetic voice as human.
- Lo-fi and personal tends to beat polished-ad in wellness, which matches the "authenticity paradox".

### Gaps
- I found no study that directly compares a human-narrated with a TTS-narrated short-form *video* on shares or retention. The evidence comes from audio and lab trust tasks.
- I found no rigorous comparison of face-on-camera vs faceless short-form health content on engagement (the thumbnail data covers YouTube long-form and is industry-sourced).

## 7. Misinformation risk and credibility signals in health content

### Takeaway
False content spreads further on average, but mostly because it gets bigger cascades, which are driven by novelty and emotion. People often share without considering accuracy, and simple accuracy prompts improve how well they discern what to share, including for COVID-19 health content. Health-video engagement on TikTok is largely decoupled from accuracy. That creates both a reputational risk and an opening for accurate, evidence-framed creators.

### Cited Findings
- Vosoughi, Roy & Aral, *Science* 2018 [PR][LD]: about 126,000 Twitter rumor cascades (2006-2017). False news reached more people, faster and deeper than the truth. False stories were about 70% more likely to be retweeted, and were more novel and evoked surprise and disgust (recalled, not re-fetched) — [Science DOI](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aap9559). Caveat: Juul & Ugander (2021) show the structural differences are mostly explained by size — [PMC8609637](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8609637)
- Pennycook, Epstein, Mosleh, Arechar, Eckles & Rand, *Nature* 2021 [PR]: whether a headline was true barely affected sharing intentions even though it strongly affected accuracy judgments, so inattention explains much of misinformation sharing. A simple accuracy prompt improved sharing discernment, including in a Twitter field experiment. The study covered US political news — [Computational Social Science digest](https://comdig.cssociety.org/2021/03/19/shifting-attention-to-accuracy-can-reduce-misinformation-online/); [Chicago Policy Review](https://chicagopolicyreview.org/2022/01/04/try-accuracy-prompts-to-reduce-the-spread-of-misinformation-online/)
- Pennycook, McPhetres, Zhang, Lu & Rand, *Psychological Science* 2020 (COVID-19): an accuracy reminder (rating one non-COVID headline) nearly tripled truth discernment in sharing intentions for COVID-19 headlines — [PMC7366427](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7366427)
- TikTok health studies (JMIR Infodemiology 2026, JMIR Dermatology 2025) find engagement is driven by style and is often unrelated to accuracy or educational quality — [JMIR Infodemiology](https://infodemiology.jmir.org/2026/1/e85323); [JMIR Dermatology](https://derma.jmir.org/2025/1/e70010)
- Shock and scare health videos drew views but often lacked factual information (O'Donnell et al. 2023) — [WSU release](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2023/07/26/viral-tiktok-health-videos-tend-to-cover-three-topics-rely-on-influencers/)

### Inferences
- Novelty and surprise drive spread whether a claim is true or false. A wellness creator can use real novelty ("a surprising thing your nervous system does") without overclaiming. Hedged, research-backed wording ("can trigger or worsen") protects credibility with skeptical viewers and with platform health-misinformation policies.
- Credentials alone do not reliably raise engagement. Pairing a relatable story with a brief credibility cue (a cited study, "research shows") is a reasonable hybrid, but it is untested.

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring how on-video credibility cues (citations on screen, "research says" captions) affect share rates of short-form wellness content.
- I found no platform-specific data on how TikTok or Instagram health-misinformation labels or demotion affect reach for borderline alternative-wellness content.
